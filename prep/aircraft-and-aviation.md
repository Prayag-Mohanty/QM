Aviation runs on naming systems with visible seams — skipped numbers, recycled designations and a nickname taken from a 1940s comic strip. The gaps are usually the question.

## Quiz-ready stories

1. **Skunk Works** is named after the "**Skonk Works**", a moonshine still in Al Capp's comic strip *Li'l Abner*. Lockheed's wartime team worked in a tent next to a smelly plastics factory; an engineer answered the phone with it and the name stuck.
2. **Boeing's 7X7** exists because the firm's internal numbering assigned the **700 block to jet transports**. The first jetliner was simply "Model 700"; marketing preferred **707**.
3. The **Boeing 717** designation was used twice: first internally for a military tanker, then finally applied decades later to the **McDonnell Douglas MD-95** that Boeing inherited in the 1997 merger.
4. **Airbus skipped the A360 and A370** and jumped to the **A380** — the 8 is lucky across much of Asia, and the double-deck cross-section looks like an 8.
5. There is **no F-13** — skipped for superstition, so the Navy's new fighter became the **F-14 Tomcat**. There is no **F-19** either: Northrop asked for it to be skipped to avoid confusion with the **MiG-19**.
6. **Soviet aircraft are named after their chief designers**: Antonov, Tupolev, Ilyushin, Sukhoi. **MiG** is two names — **Mikoyan** and **Gurevich**.
7. The **An-225**, the largest aircraft ever built, was named **Mriya** — Ukrainian for "**Dream**".
8. **Bombardier** began in **1942** as a **snowmobile** maker; its founder built his first snow vehicle to get his sick son to hospital through a Quebec winter.
9. **Marcel Dassault** was born **Marcel Bloch**. He took the name after WWII from his brother's Resistance code name, "**Char d'Assaut**" (assault tank).
10. **Grumman** was nicknamed the "**Iron Works**" for its rugged naval fighters, all named after **cats**: Wildcat, Hellcat, Tigercat, Bearcat, and later the Tomcat.

## US fighter designations

### The system

Before 1962 the Air Force and Navy ran **separate** schemes, and the same aircraft could be the Navy's **F4H Phantom II** and the Air Force's **F-110 Spectre**. Defense Secretary **Robert McNamara** forced a unified **Tri-Service Designation System** in 1962; fighters restarted at **F-1** and have counted up since.

### The gaps

| Number | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **F-13** | Skipped for superstition — it would have gone to the **F-14 Tomcat**. "-13" is now always skipped across US military aircraft. |
| **F-19** | **Northrop asked for it to be skipped**, worried about confusion with the **MiG-19**. The long-running myth that it was reserved for a secret stealth fighter is just a myth. |
| **F-20 Tigershark** | Northrop specifically wanted an **even** number, to stand apart from the odd-numbered Soviet jets. |
| **F-23** | The **YF-23 Black Widow II** lost the Advanced Tactical Fighter contest to the **F-22 Raptor** and was never built beyond prototypes. |
| **F-24 to F-34** | Never assigned to anything. |
| **F-35** | The Joint Strike Fighter contenders were **X-plane** prototypes — Boeing's X-32 and Lockheed's **X-35**. The winner simply kept its prototype number instead of taking the next fighter slot. |

### The F-47

The Air Force's sixth-generation **NGAD** fighter was awarded to **Boeing** in **March 2025** and designated the **F-47**. The Air Force's stated justification is threefold: it echoes the WWII **P-47 Thunderbolt**, it marks the Air Force's founding year (**1947**), and its own release says it recognises the **47th president's** support for the programme. FOIA-obtained emails later reported by Bloomberg showed officials drafting that historical justification **after** the number had been announced publicly.

First flight is targeted for **2028**, with roughly 200 aircraft expected in service in the mid-2030s. The Navy's parallel sixth-generation programme, **F/A-XX**, has a winner picked between Boeing and Northrop Grumman but no publicly announced designation yet.
