## What six game weeks teach

This guide is built from six game weeks of **BBQL (Business Brain Quiz League) Season 3**: 288 questions in 72 quads of four. Each week has 12 themes, shuffled across 6 rounds. Three lessons hold across all six weeks:

1. **Business-adjacent beats business core.** Only about a fifth of quads are classic company or finance history. The rest are names, marketing, places, people and culture with a money angle: Broadway grosses, Bollywood money songs, art auctions, fictional idle rich.
2. **Five of the six weeks have a current-affairs quad**, on news from the previous four to eight weeks. Daily news reading is not optional.
3. **Spot the quad, then exploit the gradient.** Question .1 is usually the gimme and .4 the stretch. Once you know the theme, the long tail is what wins points.

One caution: the quad labels are not always clean. In GW5, the Martha Stewart and flying-money questions sit under each other's quad numbers. Trust the content over the label.

**Quad by quad: the pattern, then what to learn next**

1. **Ads.** Identify the brand from a described or played ad. Next: Coca-Cola's polar bears date to its 1993 "Northern Lights" ad, which is why Pepsi's 2026 version was a jab; Avocados From Mexico has been a Super Bowl regular since 2015; know Apple's "1984" and Budweiser's "Wassup".
2. **Mc- companies.** A shared prefix plus a "Scottish descent" clue. Next: McVitie's and McIlhenny (Tabasco), the set's spares; McLaren (Bruce McLaren, a New Zealander); McKinsey (James O. McKinsey, American, so not a Scottish-founder answer).
3. **Famous dresses.** A red-carpet moment, name the wearer or house. Next: Marilyn Monroe's white subway-grate dress (*The Seven Year Itch*), Audrey Hepburn's Givenchy black dress (*Breakfast at Tiffany's*), Elizabeth Hurley's Versace safety-pin dress (1994), Björk's swan dress (2001 Oscars).
4. **Land reclamation.** An engineered site, name the place. Next: Flevoland (the Netherlands' reclaimed province), Hong Kong's Chek Lap Kok airport, Mumbai's seven islands joined into one city.
5. **2026 news.** See the current-affairs digest. Next: the 2026 economics Nobel (Hall and Woodford) and Bangladesh's pending LDC deferral vote are the obvious follow-ups.
6. **Celebrity and musician brands.** The founder is the clue, the brand the answer. Next: Fenty Beauty (Rihanna), Skims (Kim Kardashian), Aviation Gin (Ryan Reynolds, sold to Diageo), Teremana (Dwayne Johnson), Beats (Dr. Dre). Rickenbacker hints at a sub-theme: guitar brands named after people, such as Fender (Leo Fender) and Gibson (Orville Gibson).
7. **FIFA 2026 sponsors.** A sponsor's odd placement or subsidiary. Next: Adidas has supplied the World Cup ball since 1970; Coca-Cola has been a FIFA partner since 1978; 2026 is the first 48-team World Cup, co-hosted by the US, Canada and Mexico.
8. **Hidden meanings in logos.** Name the company or what the hidden shape shows. Next: FedEx's arrow, Amazon's A-to-Z smile, Baskin-Robbins' 31, the Tour de France cyclist, Carrefour's C, Hyundai's handshake H, Toblerone's bear.
9. **Secret formulas.** A vault or recipe story, name the brand or the custodian. Next: Chartreuse (recipe held by two monks), Dr Pepper's 23 flavours, Jägermeister's 56 botanicals; WD-40 stands for Water Displacement, 40th formula.
10. **Early search engines.** A naming or tech-first clue. Next: Lycos (named after the wolf spider), Excite, Infoseek, HotBot; Google began as BackRub.
11. **Bottled water.** Source plus a design or celebrity clue. Next: Bisleri (an Italian brand bought by the Chauhans), Kinley (Coca-Cola), Aquafina (PepsiCo), Voss, Vittel, Gerolsteiner.
12. **Amul topicals.** Read the pun, name the news it riffs on. Next: Amul stands for Anand Milk Union Limited; the Amul girl dates to 1966 (daCunha); Verghese Kurien and Operation Flood are the history quad waiting to happen.

**Quad by quad: the pattern, then what to learn next**

1. **Prizes funded by tycoons.** The prize is described, the money behind it asked. Next: the Booker (from Booker McConnell, a sugar trader in British Guiana), the Kyoto Prize (Kazuo Inamori of Kyocera), the Kluge Prize, the Infosys Prize, the Nobel itself (dynamite money).

3. **Go- companies.** A shared prefix with founder and pivot clues. Next: Go First (formerly GoAir, the Wadia group's airline), Goibibo, and GoTo, the 2021 merger of Gojek and Tokopedia; Gojek founder Nadiem Makarim later became Indonesia's education minister.

8. **Venture capital.** A firm's motto, programme or founder. Next: Kleiner Perkins, Accel, Benchmark, Tiger Global; Sequoia India became Peak XV in 2023; Marc Andreessen's "Why Software Is Eating the World" (2011).
9. **Economics Nobels.** Laureate anecdotes and firsts. Next: Frisch and Tinbergen were the inaugural 1969 winners; Amartya Sen (1998); Elinor Ostrom (2009, first woman); Banerjee and Duflo (2019); Claudia Goldin (2023, first woman to win alone); Hall and Woodford (2026).
10. **Business schools named for donors.** A donor's fortune, name the school. Next: Sloan (the spare), Haas (Walter Haas of Levi Strauss), Ross, Fuqua, Johnson (Cornell); in India JBIMS (Jamnalal Bajaj), SPJIMR (S.P. Jain), IIT Bombay's SJMSOM (Shailesh J. Mehta).
11. **Idle rich in fiction.** A character of independent means, name them. Next: Bruce Wayne, Tony Stark, Richie Rich, Scrooge McDuck, the Crawleys of *Downton Abbey*.
12. **Asian currency names.** An etymology clue, name the country and currency. Next: yen, yuan and won all mean "round"; riyal and real come from "royal"; rupee from Sanskrit for wrought silver; the kina (Papua New Guinea) from pearl shells.

The chart is the real lesson: a business quiz is less about companies than about the vocabulary, places and people around them.

**Question styles that recur**

- **Convergent biography.** Three or four career facts that only fit one person (Tom Peters: McKinsey, *In Search of Excellence*, Nixon drug-policy job, admitted fudged data).
- **Story to brand.** A design or campaign anecdote with the brand name withheld (Tropicana's 2009 redesign, Toblerone's Matterhorn).
- **Blank with a letter count.** "\[BLANK\] King (7)" → Railway. The count is a big hint; always check it.
- **Name the country.** A company or institution described, the country asked. Pure recall of HQs.
- **Double hop.** The question names a brand, the answer is a term named after it (Droste cocoa → Droste effect; Osborne Computer → Osborne effect).
- **Audio-visual.** The Super Bowl quad ran as 20-second video clips.

## GW6: theme deep dives

Each quad below has its pattern, then the material a setter is most likely to reach for next. If you know the extension list, the .3 and .4 questions of a similar quad become .1s.

### 1. Viral brand social media

**Pattern:** one famous campaign or tweet, identify the brand.

- **Old Spice**: "The Man Your Man Could Smell Like" (2010, Isaiah Mustafa, Wieden+Kennedy).
- **KFC**: its Twitter account followed only 11 accounts, the 5 Spice Girls and 6 men named Herb (11 herbs and spices).
- **Planters** killed off Mr. Peanut in 2020. Duolingo's 2025 owl "death" copied the playbook.
- **Burger King**: the Moldy Whopper (2020), advertising no artificial preservatives.
- **Wendy's** before Twitter: "Where's the beef?" (1984, Clara Peller).
- **Amul**: topical hoardings since 1966, Amul girl by Sylvester daCunha's agency.
- **Dove** Real Beauty (2004), **Coca-Cola** Share a Coke (2011, Australia first), the **ALS Ice Bucket Challenge** (2014).

### 2. Business terms named after a product or thing

**Pattern:** a definition plus an origin story; the answer is an eponym. This is the highest-yield quad type in business quizzing.

- **Takeover defences** (extend Pac-Man): poison pill (Martin Lipton, 1982), white knight, grey knight, golden parachute, crown jewel, scorched earth, shark repellent, greenmail, bear hug, macaroni defence (bonds whose redemption value expands on takeover).
- **Effects and laws**: Streisand effect (2003 Malibu house photo), Cobra effect (British bounty on cobras in Delhi), Lindy effect (Lindy's deli, New York), Diderot effect, IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon, Ariely, 2011), Dutch disease (Groningen gas, coined by *The Economist* in 1977), Parkinson's law, Peter principle, Goodhart's law.
- **Indices with a funny name**: Big Mac Index (*The Economist*, 1986), Lipstick index (Leonard Lauder), Hemline index.
- **Food physics** (extend Cheerios): Brazil nut effect (big particles rise when shaken), coffee ring effect, Mpemba effect.
- **Genericised trademarks**: Aspirin, Escalator, Thermos, Zipper, Trampoline and Heroin (a Bayer trademark) all lost brand status. Xerox and Google fight it.

### 3. UN Least Developed Countries

**Pattern:** a country identified from its LDC history plus one economic fact.

- Category created 1971, reviewed every three years by the UN Committee for Development Policy on three tests: income per head, a human assets index and an economic vulnerability index.
- **44 LDCs today**: 32 in Africa, 8 in Asia, 3 in the Pacific (Kiribati, Solomon Islands, Tuvalu) and Haiti.
- **Graduated, in order**: Botswana 1994, Cabo Verde 2007, Maldives 2011, Samoa 2014, Equatorial Guinea 2017, Vanuatu 2020, Bhutan 2023, São Tomé and Príncipe 2024. Sikkim left the list in 1975 by joining India.
- **Pipeline**: Bangladesh, Laos and Nepal are due to graduate on 24 November 2026. Bangladesh and Nepal asked for three more years, to November 2029. The CDP backed it and ECOSOC sent it to the General Assembly; the final vote is pending as of late October 2026. Solomon Islands follows in December 2027, Cambodia and Senegal in 2029.
- LDC conferences: Paris 1981 and 1991, Brussels 2001, Istanbul 2011, Doha 2023.

### 4. Building nicknames

**Pattern:** describe the shape, ask for the nickname. Best when the building has a business story.

- **London**: the Gherkin (30 St Mary Axe), the Walkie-Talkie (20 Fenchurch Street, nicknamed "Walkie-Scorchie" after its glare melted parts of a parked Jaguar in 2013), the Cheesegrater (Leadenhall Building), the Scalpel.
- **New York**: the Lipstick Building, where Bernie Madoff ran his fund.
- **Montreal**: the Olympic Stadium, "the Big O", mocked as "the Big Owe" because its debt took 30 years to clear.
- **Beijing**: CCTV headquarters, "Big Pants". **Prague**: Dancing House, "Fred and Ginger".
- **Shanghai**: Bottle Opener (World Financial Center) next to the twisting Shanghai Tower.

### 5. News and media founders

**Pattern:** convergent biography of a media pioneer.

- **The 19th-century news cartel**: Havas (Paris, 1835), Wolff (Berlin, 1849) and Reuters (London, 1851) carved up the world's news markets by treaty.
- **Associated Press** (1846): New York papers pooled costs to cover the Mexican-American War.
- **Joseph Pulitzer vs William Randolph Hearst**: the circulation war that gave "yellow journalism" its name (from the Yellow Kid comic).
- **Adolph Ochs** gave the *New York Times* "All the News That's Fit to Print".
- **Henry Luce** (with Briton Hadden): *Time* 1923, *Fortune* 1930, *Life* 1936.
- **Charles Dow, Edward Jones, Charles Bergstresser**: Dow Jones (1882), *Wall Street Journal* (1889).
- **Michael Bloomberg**: left Salomon Brothers and built the terminal company in 1981.
- **India**: Ramnath Goenka (*Indian Express*), Subhash Chandra (Zee TV, 1992, the first private Hindi satellite channel), Prannoy and Radhika Roy (NDTV, Adani took control in 2022), PTI (took over Reuters' Indian arm in 1949).

### 8. Patent wars

**Pattern:** the litigants are given, the invention is asked.

- **Edison vs Swan**: the light bulb, settled by forming Ediswan in Britain.
- **Marconi vs Tesla**: radio; the US Supreme Court invalidated Marconi's key patent in 1943.

- **Novartis vs Union of India** (2013): Glivec, Section 3(d) and evergreening.
- **Patent pools**: the 1856 sewing machine combination, the 1917 Manufacturers Aircraft Association that ended the Wright-Curtiss fight.

## How to crack each question type

Most misses come from the attack, not the knowledge. Each archetype has a tell in its wording and a best first move.

| Archetype | Tell in the wording | First move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eponym or named term | "what effect / defence / term, named after" | Name the product in the clue first, then attach the suffix. Droste cocoa makes Droste effect. |
| Corporate legal name | "legal name", "parent entity", "registered as" | The setter almost always plants wordplay. Decode it (Adi to ANI, 197 to One97, founder initials to FSN) instead of trying to recall. |
| Name the country | an institution described, "in which country" | Use the proper nouns: Pantai is Malay, Acibadem is Turkish, Helios sits under Fresenius of Bad Homburg. |
| Fill the blank (N) | "\[BLANK\]" plus a letter count | Count first. The blank is usually a word in a famous phrase (Railway King, Bubble Act). |
| Convergent biography | four career facts, "name this person" | Start from the rarest clue (Nixon drug-policy adviser), not the first one read. |
| Audio-visual | a 20-second clip | Look for the original actor in the ad; the casting gives the film. |
| Quad recognition | the second question on a theme | Keep a running list of themes seen. After two hits, pre-list the likely remaining answers. |

**Answer form matters.** GW6 accepted only the exact two words "Turning Torso" and asked for legal names, not brands. Give the form the question asks for, and in a pounce-and-bounce format check before you lock.

**Archetypes added by GW1–GW5**

| Archetype | Tell in the wording | First move |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common-name connection | "identify the common surname", "name's the same" | Solve the easiest clue alone, then test the name on the others. |
| Country from data | a block of GDP, per-capita and richest-person figures | Read the richest person first; the GDP gap or one export usually confirms it. |
| Both parts needed | "brand and actress", "word and what was added" | Lock the easier part, then spend the remaining time on the second. |
| Recent news | "in recent news", "based on a recent launch" | Only daily reading helps; see the digest above. |
| Prefix or family set | the second Mc- or Go- answer of a round | Pre-list every company with that prefix and match the remaining clues against it. |

## Preparation system

The goal is to own themes, not facts. A quad-style quiz rewards someone who has seen a theme's whole long tail, so prep in themes, write questions yourself, and review misses as themes.

### Weekly routine

| When | Task | Time |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | Read one business newsletter (Finshots, Mint, ET Brand Equity). Log 3 facts that could be a question, each tagged with a theme. | 20 min |
| Daily | Anki review: legal names, HQs, eponyms, LDC and other fixed lists. | 10 min |
| Weekly | Play one past set under real timing (a BBQL set runs about 45 minutes). | 45 min |
| Weekly | Post-mortem every miss: which quad it belonged to, and 3 extensions you didn't know. | 30 min |
| Weekly | Write one quad of your own, four questions graded .1 to .4. Setting is the fastest way to learn what setters reach for. | 45 min |
| Monthly | Deep-dive one evergreen theme from the list below, with a Wikipedia list page as the spine. | 2 h |

### Evergreen themes to build next

The season map covers what BBQL has used so far. These themes also recur across Crucible, BizQuiz and other league sets:

- **Names**: company name etymologies, legal names, founders' first ventures, mascots, stock tickers (Harley-Davidson is HOG, Southwest is LUV).
- **Marketing**: logos with hidden symbols, taglines, ad agencies and their campaigns, product flops (New Coke, Google Glass), rebrands that failed.
- **Deals**: famous M&A, failed mergers, takeover jargon, IPOs and listings, family business groups (Tata, Birla, Bajaj, Godrej).
- **Money**: bubbles and scams, central banks and currencies, economic terms and indices, fintech and UPI.
- **People**: management gurus, business books, CEO successions, consulting alumni, Nobel economists.

### In the room

- **Spot quads early.** After two questions on one theme, list the likely remaining answers on paper.
- **Respect negative marking late.** In a close final, a wrong buzz in the last round costs more than a pass. Buzz only past roughly 70% confidence when a neg would hand over the lead.
- **Use the rare clue.** In long convergent questions, the rarest fact is the one that ends the search.
- **Answer in the asked form.** Legal name, two-word nickname, surname in capitals: the setter decides what is accepted.
